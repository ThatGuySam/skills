# Historical-to-modern policy

This policy is the guardrail between Emily Post's public-domain writing and present-day advice. Apply it before answering, not as a disclaimer added afterward.

## Evidence classes

| Class | Meaning | Allowed use |
|---|---|---|
| `primary-advice` | Emily Post's 1922 *Etiquette* | Direct historical evidence; adapt before prescribing |
| `adjacent-advice` | Emily Post's 1930 home/design guidance | Context for hospitality, comfort, suitability, and taste; lower authority |
| `style-only` | Post's fiction or travel writing | Voice study only; never etiquette doctrine |
| `current-tradition` | Original summary of a current Emily Post Institute page | Modern bridge; not Emily Post's own words |
| `modern-synthesis` | This skill's reasoned recommendation | Label as present-day judgment, not sourced historical fact |

## Modernity labels

- `durable`: The passage primarily protects dignity, time, comfort, privacy, trust, or choice and can transfer with little change.
- `contextual`: The underlying concern may transfer, but the prescribed form depends on culture, formality, relationship, or setting.
- `historical-only`: The rule depends on a social structure that should not be prescribed now.

One passage can contain a durable principle inside a historical-only rule. Extract the principle explicitly; do not quietly carry the old hierarchy forward.

The corpus `modernity` field is only a chapter-level triage default assigned by the builder. It is not a passage review and must never replace this per-rule classification.

## Mandatory risk checks

Check every recommendation for:

1. **Consent and autonomy** — Does anyone need a genuine, low-pressure way to decline?
2. **Power** — Could an employer, host, elder, donor, customer, romantic partner, or gatekeeper make “politeness” coercive?
3. **Inclusion** — Does the rule assume binary gender, heterosexual marriage, one religion, inherited rank, wealth, servants, or physical ability?
4. **Culture** — Is the convention specifically white, affluent, early-20th-century U.S. society rather than a portable principle?
5. **Labor** — Does graciousness for one person create invisible or uncompensated work for another?
6. **Accessibility and safety** — Would the formal rule make participation harder or less safe?
7. **Channel and privacy** — Do digital persistence, audience size, interruption, or data exposure change the social cost?
8. **Truthfulness** — Is a candid boundary kinder than an elaborate excuse or false promise?

## Recurring historical hazards

| Corpus area | Default status | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Introductions, invitations, conversation, letters | contextual with durable principles | rank precedence, gendered forms, calling-card mechanics |
| Domestic service and “well-appointed” homes | historical-only/contextual | class hierarchy, surveillance of workers, invisible labor |
| Debutantes, chaperons, courtship, engagements | historical-only | binary gender, sexual double standards, paternal control |
| Weddings and christenings | contextual | heterosexual and Christian assumptions, family rank |
| Funerals and condolence | contextual with durable care | religious uniformity, rigid mourning dress, household labor |
| Clubs, dress, pronunciation, “best society” | historical-only/contextual | exclusion, class signaling, ableism, cultural and linguistic prejudice |
| Business and politics | contextual | male-only workplace, secretary/servant assumptions, modern HR law |
| Travel and cross-cultural claims | historical-only/contextual | national stereotypes, colonial gaze, inaccessible travel assumptions |

## Conflict order

When rules conflict, use this order:

1. Safety, law, consent, accessibility, and anti-discrimination obligations
2. Honest boundaries and material burdens
3. Consideration for the most affected person, especially the less powerful one
4. Explicit host, venue, workplace, religious, or cultural expectations
5. Relationship-preserving clarity and repair
6. Formal convention and aesthetics

## Attribution language

Use exact labels:

- **Direct:** “Post advises …” followed by a citation to a Post-authored chunk.
- **Inference:** “A principle inferred from these passages is …” with at least two supporting chunks when practical.
- **Historical context:** “In the book's 1922 setting …”
- **Modern adaptation:** “For the present day, I would adapt that to …”
- **Current tradition:** “The current Emily Post Institute summarizes its approach as …” citing the modern bridge, not Emily Post herself.

Do not say a modern answer is “what Emily would say.” No source can establish that counterfactual.
